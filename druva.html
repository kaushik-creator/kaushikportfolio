Case Study · 02

This project is about how Druva, its service partners (MSPs), and end customers share one system for turning products on and controlling who can see them. The partner console could only offer a limited product set, so partners lost deals when a customer needed something outside it. I researched where the three parties' needs collided, then designed the provisioning and access model that let partners offer more — without breaking licensing rules or exposing a customer's private data to their partner.

01 · The question

Where do the partner, the customer, and Druva's own needs actually conflict — and can one access model serve all three?

MSPs lost customers when MSC couldn't provision products outside its supported set. Licensing blocked the obvious fix of adding products freely. Customer privacy added a second constraint: partners couldn't get standing visibility into every console.

02 · The method

I grounded this work in three inputs — no invented research, only what I actually had access to:

  • Live MSP admin calls — observed how partners onboard customers, assign products, and hit walls when licensing blocked an action.
  • Internal docs + Support threads — read licensing rules, provisioning dependencies, and recurring support tickets about access confusion.
  • Working sessions with PMs and engineers — mapped which state changes were technically possible, irreversible, or blocked at the API layer.

The same action — provisioning a product — followed different rules depending on who initiated it and who could see the result. That inconsistency became the research focus.

03 · The synthesis

Research didn't cluster cleanly — the same provisioning action meant different things to each party. I mapped the conflict as a trust triangle before drawing screens.

Partner wants

More access — sell and manage more products from one dashboard, with less dependency on Druva support.

Customer wants

Less exposure — data and reports stay private; no standing MSP visibility into their console.

Druva wants

Less manual work — scale provisioning without adding operational drag for every new product.

Full persona detail (goals, pain, activities) informed the triangle above — kept as research evidence, not as page filler.

From those conflicts I built a system map of actors, customer types, access models, and which state conversions were legal — including irreversible ones.

From the map I derived a four-state access model — MSC- or Druva-provisioned × Restricted or Unrestricted — so partners could read customer state at a glance.

Restricted Unrestricted
MSC-Provisioned Core products only. No advanced workloads. Full MSP access to console, accounts, and reports.
Druva-Provisioned Licenses provisioned; no console access afterward. Full access plus workloads unavailable to MSC customers.

04 · The decision

Each design choice tied back to a research finding:

Four states on the customer list

Research showed partners couldn't tell provisioning type from access level. I made both legible with badges and disabled invalid actions with a reason — not a silent failure.

Irreversibility via active friction

Some conversions can't be undone. Research flagged support tickets from accidental migrations. I used typed confirmation, not a blind modal, before irreversible state changes.

Time-boxed customer-approved access

Privacy standoff: partners needed console access; customers resisted standing visibility. I designed 24/48/72-hour requests with customer veto, auto-revoke, and enforcement at the API layer.

Three-portal handoffs

Each scenario split across MSC, S-portal, and C-portal with explicit ownership — so licensing verification and customer approval never landed on the wrong actor.

05 · The validation

Before launch I checked the design against the research inputs: stakeholder review with PM and engineering confirmed state transitions matched API constraints; Support reviewed the four-state labels against recurring ticket language.

Post-launch, the team observed directional improvement — more partner customers onboarded and higher product adoption — but I can't attribute that to this feature alone. I'd track: support tickets per provisioning action, time-to-enable a new product, and failed conversion attempts.

06 · The outcome

Self-serve expansion

Partners request and provision advanced workloads without a support ticket for every addition. The design enabled this — not a measured percentage I can claim alone.

Clearer customer management

Four states legible at a glance. Invalid conversions blocked — partners see what's allowed and where the privacy boundary sits.

Business result (directional)

After launch, MSC brought on more partner customers and saw higher product adoption — team-observed, not per-feature attributed. I enabled the self-serve path; I didn't isolate the metric.

Less manual provisioning

Druva Support handled fewer licensing-restricted provisioning requests — the time-boxed access model replaced standing console grants.
